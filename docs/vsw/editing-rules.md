# VSW Blog Editing Rules

These rules are extracted from director (Mark Ponce) feedback on actual VSW blog drafts. Every draft must pass through these rules before being presented. They represent the gap between "first draft" and "publish-ready."

---

## Rule 1: Lead with the hook, not the logistics

**Bad**: "Vancouver Startup Week is back this April 2026, and if you have ever built anything in this city, you already know why that matters."
**Good**: "Save the dates: April 27 to May 1 because Vancouver Startup Week (VSW) is back!"

The date and the energy come first. Don't bury the when. Don't make the reader wait for the point. Open with something that makes someone want to keep reading or mark their calendar.

## Rule 2: Use brand language, not generic language

**Bad**: "a steady point in BC's startup scene"
**Good**: "the heartbeat of BC's startup scene"

VSW has a brand messaging framework. Use it. "Heartbeat" is a core brand word. "Steady point" is forgettable. When there's a brand-approved phrase that fits, use it instead of inventing a weaker synonym.

## Rule 3: Simpler is tighter

**Bad**: "brings founders into the same rooms for conversations"
**Good**: "brings founders together for conversations"

Cut words that add length without adding meaning. "Into the same rooms" sounds specific but adds nothing. "Together" says the same thing faster.

## Rule 4: Don't front-load logistics

**Bad (early in post)**: "Vancouver Startup Week 2026 runs April 27 to May 01. Early bird details are coming soon."
**Good**: Save the dates and logistics for the opening hook and the closing CTA only.

Logistics in the middle of a narrative kill momentum. The reader knows the dates from the hook. Don't repeat them until the closing ask.

## Rule 5: Event lists use possessive form, not colon format

**Bad**: "Toast: Men in Tech 2.0" / "Angel Forum: Venture Financing 101"
**Good**: "Toast's Men in Tech 2.0" / "Angel Forum's Venture Financing 101"

Possessive reads more natural and editorial. Colons read like a program schedule. The exception is when the format is "Org A and Org B: Event Name" (two co-hosts).

## Rule 6: Don't self-reference VSW in event lists

**Bad**: "VSW 2025: Opening Reception" / "VSW 2025: Closing Party at D/6"
**Good**: "The Opening Reception" / "The Closing Party at D/6"

When the entire blog is about VSW, you don't need to label every item as a VSW event. It's redundant. Use "The" instead.

## Rule 7: Flow over fragmentation

**Bad**: "Yes, sessions filled up. Yes, big topics were covered. But the real signal was what happened in between."
**Good**: "Yes, sessions filled up, big topics were covered, but the real signal was what happened in between."

Short staccato sentences can feel punchy in your head but read as choppy on the page. When three short sentences are building toward a single point, merge them into one flowing sentence with commas.

## Rule 8: Don't fabricate specifics

**Bad**: "You will overhear someone work through a messy hiring lesson."
**Good**: (Cut it entirely, or replace with something verifiable)

If you didn't witness it, don't write it. Fabricated "scene-setting" details feel inauthentic. Either reference something real or cut it. This is especially important for the "Connection is the Point" sections where it's tempting to paint a picture.

## Rule 9: Precise audience language

**Bad**: "For a lot of people in BC"
**Good**: "For many entrepreneurs in BC"

"A lot of people" is vague. "Many entrepreneurs" is specific and speaks directly to the audience. Always use the most precise descriptor for the audience segment you're addressing.

## Rule 10: Section order follows the reader's journey

The reader's journey is:
1. Why should I care? (hook)
2. Am I in the right place? (community)
3. What happened before? (proof / social proof)
4. What's the vibe? (connection)
5. What's the theme this year? (celebration / homegrown)
6. What will I actually experience? (what to expect)
7. How do I get involved? (CTA)

Don't lead with "Celebrating Homegrown Success" — that's a theme, not a hook. Lead with community ("A Community That Keeps Showing Up") because that's what makes someone feel included before you tell them what to expect.

## Rule 11: Sponsor sections earn their weight

**Bad**: "Empower BC builders at VSW. Sponsor Vancouver Startup Week."
**Good**: "Put Your Brand on the Global Stage. With thousands of attendees ranging from early-stage founders to global investors, VSW 2026 offers unparalleled access to the decision-makers of tomorrow."

Sponsor CTAs need to sell the value proposition, not just state the ask. Tell them what they get: audience size, audience quality, exposure type, and the prestige of association.

## Rule 12: No separate "Open Invitation" section

**Bad**: Having a standalone "## An open invitation" section with its own header
**Good**: The closing invitation flows naturally from the "What to Expect" section

The closing should feel like a natural crescendo, not a new section. The invitation is the emotional climax of the blog — don't box it into a labeled section.

## Rule 13: No em dashes

Never use em dashes (—). Restructure with commas, periods, colons, or break into two sentences. This is a VSW Director Feedback Rule that applies to all VSW content.

## Rule 14: Bold formatting for emphasis, not decoration

Bold should highlight:
- Key stats ("**5,000 attendees, 200 speakers, and 100 event partners**")
- Proper nouns in lists ("**Vancouver Tech Journal**, **Osler**, **RBCx**")
- Core taglines ("**create space for people to meet, learn, and build forward together**")

Bold should NOT be used for:
- Entire sentences or paragraphs
- Every mention of a company name after the first
- Section headers (these are already visually distinct via markdown)

## Rule 15: Quotes in sponsor blogs must be real

Every quote attributed to a person in a sponsor blog must come from an actual interview or provided material. Never fabricate quotes. If you don't have enough quotes, note what's missing in the brief and ask Tej to follow up with the sponsor.

## Rule 16: Research claims must be grounded

If you cite a stat (e.g., "Vancouver is home to nearly fifteen hundred startups"), it must be verifiable or come from the brief/provided material. Don't invent numbers. If you need a stat and don't have one, leave a [STAT NEEDED] placeholder.

## Rule 17: The VSW CTA checklist

Every blog must end with at minimum:
- VSW dates: April 27 to May 1, 2026
- Website or newsletter: www.vanstartupweek.ca
- Sponsor contact (if sponsor section included): connect@vanstartupweek.ca

## Rule 18: No thesis-antithesis patterns

**Bad**: "VSW isn't just a conference; it's a movement." / "It's not about the pitch; it's about the people." / "This isn't networking; it's community."
**Good**: "VSW brings the entire startup community together for five days of real connection."

Never use "it's not X; it's Y" or "not just X, but Y" constructions. This includes all dialectical hedging, concessive frameworks, rhetorical equivocation, and contrast-based reasoning. These patterns are overfit from training data and read as lazy, formulaic rhetoric. Say what something IS directly. If you find yourself writing "it's not about..." or "this isn't just...", delete the antithesis clause and lead with the positive statement.

## Rule 19: Never use "real" as an intensifier

**Bad**: "the real story of the people who were there" / "that's what makes the case real" / "real connection"
**Good**: "the story of the people who were there" / "that's what strengthens the case" / "genuine connection"

"Real" is an AI signal word. It reads as hollow emphasis and adds no meaning — if something is a story, calling it a "real story" doesn't make it more so. Cut it entirely or restructure with specific language. "Genuine" (from the brand framework) is acceptable as a deliberate substitute.
